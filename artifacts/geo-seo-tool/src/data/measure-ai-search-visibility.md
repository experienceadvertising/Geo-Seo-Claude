If you want to know whether your site appears in AI answers, start with the data the search providers actually give you. Check the new generative AI performance report in Google Search Console and AI Performance in Bing Webmaster Tools. Then compare those visibility signals with visits and real business actions on your site. Keep a small, repeatable set of customer questions for manual checks, but do not turn one screenshot into a market-share claim.

That gives you four different questions to answer:

1. Can search systems find and index the page?
2. Is the page appearing or being cited in supported AI experiences?
3. Are people arriving at the site from those experiences?
4. Do those visitors take a useful action?

Those answers belong in separate columns. An impression is not a click. A citation is not a lead. A lead is not a sale.

## Start with a page and a customer question

Pick one page that matters to your business, such as a product comparison, service explanation, or practical guide. Write down the question a buyer would ask before reaching that page. Then check whether the page answers the question clearly, states its limitations, and points to a sensible next step.

For example, an agency could test a page explaining how it manages affiliate recruitment. The customer question might be, "How do I find affiliates for a new brand?" The page should explain the research process, evaluation criteria, outreach work, and what the agency actually does. A vague promise to "grow your program" gives both readers and search systems little to work with.

This page-and-question pairing matters because a sitewide visibility number can rise while the pages that generate customers remain invisible. Track your important pages individually.

Zyppy Signal's Cyrus Shepard recommends starting with a topic for which a page already has search visibility, then checking the related questions an AI system might explore before measuring the result. His [fan-out framework](https://signal.zyppy.com/p/fan-out-framework) is useful for choosing pages and follow-up questions. He also notes that fan-out queries vary between runs, so treat the framework as a research method, not a fixed list of phrases or a promise that covering them will earn a citation. For this measurement exercise, start with your own Search Console queries and customer questions before adding speculative prompts.

## Check Google's generative AI report

Google announced dedicated Search Console performance reports for generative AI features in Search and Discover and said the rollout reached all websites worldwide by August 31, 2026. The report shows impressions, pages, countries, devices for Search, and dates. It is a visibility report. It does not, by itself, tell you which impression became a qualified lead.

Record the report's date range and the pages that appear. Compare them with the same date range later. If a useful page has no AI impressions, first inspect its ordinary Search eligibility and whether its content answers the intended question. Google says supporting links in AI Overviews and AI Mode need normal Search eligibility with a snippet. There is no separate technical requirement or special AI markup that guarantees inclusion.

Google's overall Search performance and analytics data still have separate jobs. Search Console shows search impressions, clicks, queries, and pages. Analytics shows what visitors do after arriving. Compare trends, but do not expect their counts to match exactly.

## Check Bing's AI Performance report

Bing Webmaster Tools has an AI Performance report for supported Microsoft Copilot, Bing AI summaries, and selected partner experiences. It shows cited pages and grounding-query context. Use it to find which pages are actually cited and which topics prompt those citations. Do not interpret a citation count as a ranking position or as proof that a page influenced a purchase.

If your important page is absent, compare it with pages on your own site that do appear. Is it indexable? Does it answer a more specific question? Does it show the product, method, source, or evidence clearly? Those are practical inspection questions, not a formula for forcing a citation.

## Measure visits and outcomes separately

In your analytics, review traffic attributed to the AI and search sources you can identify. Check landing pages and whether visitors complete a useful action such as a qualified inquiry, signup, or purchase. Some AI experiences may not pass a clean referral label, so treat unattributed visits as unknown rather than assigning them to AI.

Keep a simple monthly table:

For each page, keep these fields in one row of your working sheet:

- Page URL and the customer question it should answer.
- Google AI impressions and Bing citations, recorded separately with the reporting date range.
- Identifiable visits to that page and qualified actions from analytics or your CRM.
- Notes on content changes, tracking changes, and anything that makes the period unusual.

For example, a service page might pair with the question "How does this service work?" Leave the provider and outcome fields blank until you can read their values.
Only fill a cell with a value you can read from that source. A blank or unavailable report is not zero performance. If your analytics and CRM disagree about a lead, investigate the event and handoff before making a content decision.

## Use manual AI-answer checks carefully

A recurring question set can reveal problems that dashboards miss, such as an old product description or a competitor comparison that omits an important qualification. Choose questions from customer conversations, Search Console queries, and sales objections. Save the exact question, date, location or account context, answer, cited URLs, and whether your brand was mentioned accurately.

Keep the set stable enough to compare over time. AI answers can vary by prompt, model, location, and date, so a single run is an observation, not a market-wide visibility score. When an answer is wrong, trace the claim to your own page, third-party sources, or missing evidence before rewriting the site.

## Decide what to improve next

Use the evidence to choose one page-level action. If the page is not indexed, fix access and indexing first. If it is indexed but does not answer the buyer's question well, strengthen the explanation, product details, examples, and source links. If it appears in AI features but produces no meaningful visits or actions, inspect the page's next step and whether the audience is relevant. Avoid publishing several near-identical articles just because one AI answer did not cite you.

AEO Improvement can help you audit a page and organize the work. Start with a page that matters to the business, review each recommendation against the page and provider evidence, make the change, and compare the same page and customer question again later. The goal is a better answer and a clearer customer path. Any increase in visibility or business results needs to be measured, not assumed.
