Here's my review of both the AEO/GEO/LLM audit and the Prompt Simulation for stripe.com.
Audit Review (AEO Score: 61)
What the tool got right
The audit's diagnostic framework is solid. It correctly identifies that Stripe's homepage leads with marketing copy ("Financial infrastructure to grow your revenue") rather than a definitional answer, which is genuinely the kind of opening that LLMs struggle to extract as a citeable snippet. The component breakdown (Citability 39, Brand Authority 90, Content Quality 68, Technical SEO 90, Structured Data 0, Platform Opt 65) reflects real weak spots — especially the missing Organization/FAQPage schema, which is a legitimate gap. The brand-authority checks against Wikipedia, DuckDuckGo, and GitHub are a nice touch and add credibility to the score.
Where the recommendations feel off for this specific brand
Several "industry consensus" recommendations are applied too mechanically and don't really fit a homepage like Stripe's:
The "add a named-author byline with credentials" recommendation makes sense for blog posts and articles but is awkward on a corporate homepage — no major payments company puts "By Jane Doe, VP of Payments" on stripe.com, and Claude/ChatGPT don't actually penalize homepages for this. Same with the "Last Updated: May 2025" suggestion — homepages typically don't carry publish dates, and forcing one can look strange.
The "add 2–5 outbound links to .gov/.edu sources" recommendation feels generic. It's good advice for editorial content, but Stripe's homepage linking out to Federal Reserve statistics would be unusual. The audit treats homepages and articles as if they should follow identical rules.
The citability block grading is harsh and somewhat noisy. Grading the line "currencies and payment methods supported in payments volume processed in 2025…" as an F passage is technically correct (it's a fragment from a stats module) but it's not really a "passage" — it's a graphic caption. The tool seems to be parsing UI text as prose.
The auto-generated llms.txt file is useful as a starting template, but it embeds the Executive Summary verbatim under "What we do," which isn't appropriate — that section is meant to describe the business, not the audit's findings. That looks like a bug.
The JSON-LD it generates is reasonable but minimal — sameAs is an empty array, which defeats half the purpose of Organization schema. A useful tool would pre-populate Wikipedia, LinkedIn, Crunchbase, and GitHub links since it already verified those entities.
Strongest recommendations worth acting on
The three most valuable ones, in my opinion: (1) lead with a 40–60 word "Stripe is…" definition block, (2) add Organization + FAQPage schema (the score of 0 here is real and easy to fix), and (3) add a genuine FAQ section answering "What is Stripe?", "How does Stripe compare to PayPal/Square/Adyen?", and use-case questions. These three are well-supported by current LLM-citation research.
Prompt Simulation Review (AI Visibility Score: 45)
The simulation ran 24 queries (6 prompts × 4 engines) and produced surprisingly useful data.
Headline results
Mention rate is strong — Perplexity 100%, ChatGPT 83%, Claude 67%, Gemini 60% (with a 17% error rate, including a 90-second timeout on one query). But the citation rate is the real story: ChatGPT 0%, Perplexity 0%, Claude 33%, Gemini 60%. Stripe gets talked about constantly but rarely gets linked to as a source. That's the actionable insight.
Why this matters more than the audit score
The mention/cite gap exposes the core AEO problem better than the 61/100 score does. LLMs already know Stripe exists — they don't need stripe.com to learn about it. They cite third parties (Postman's blog, Wise, Schematic HQ, Withorb) when explaining payments topics. The "most-mentioned brands" panel — wise.com ×4, postman.com ×2, withorb.com ×2, schematichq.com ×2 — is genuinely useful competitive intel: those are the domains winning citations on Stripe's territory.
Useful feedback for the tool
The auto-generated prompts are good and category-relevant ("best payment processors for startups with global customers and developer APIs," "PCI compliance when building custom checkout," etc.). The per-prompt breakdown showing the actual model responses is the most valuable feature — you can see exactly how each engine frames Stripe vs. competitors.
A few weaknesses worth flagging:
The "Avg depth" metric (16%, 8%, 35%, 13%) isn't explained anywhere visible — it's unclear whether that's position in the response, share of word count, or something else. Definitions matter for these scores.
The Gemini timeout (one prompt fully errored) inflates noise in the score. The tool should probably retry or exclude errored queries from the percentage calculations rather than counting them as zeros.
The "Cited 0%" for ChatGPT and Perplexity might be misleading — Perplexity's responses include numbered footnote citations like [1][2][3], and the tool may not be parsing those as citations to stripe.com specifically. Worth verifying whether "Cited" means "stripe.com appears as a source link" vs. "any citation present." If the former, the metric is accurate but should be labeled "Cited (your domain)."
Bottom line
The audit is a reasonable starting framework but applies article-style recommendations to a homepage somewhat indiscriminately. The Prompt Simulation is the more valuable feature — the mention-vs-citation gap and competitor-domain panel give Stripe a clearer roadmap (publish citeable content that AI engines will link to, not just talk about) than the 61 score does. Audit Review (AEO Score: 50)
What worked well
The diagnostic was sharper this time and felt more useful than the Stripe audit. The tool correctly identified that Notion's homepage opens with "Developers: Get a first look…" and "Trusted by 98% of the Forbes Cloud 100" — neither of which tells an LLM what Notion is. The recommendation to add a Wikipedia-style opening defining Notion and Custom Agents is genuinely actionable. The executive summary was crisper here, with the proposed rewrite ("Notion is an AI workspace platform that combines docs, wikis, project management, and Custom Agents…") being a drop-in suggestion a marketing team could actually use. That's the kind of concrete output a paying customer would value.
The component scores also tell a clearer story: Citability 41, Brand Authority 56, Content Quality 46, Technical SEO 90, Structured Data 0, Platform Opt 63. The "Content Quality 46" partly reflects the page being only 517 words — a real issue for a homepage trying to compete in AI search.
Where the tool stumbled
The brand authority check failed in a way that should be embarrassing for a tool charging real money. It reported: "Wikipedia: Article 'Notion (EP)' (resolved from disambiguation 'Notion'). FOUND." That's the Wikipedia article for a music EP, not Notion the software company. There's a clear "Notion (productivity software)" Wikipedia article that the disambiguation resolver should have picked. Same issue with GitHub — it reported "@notion — 10 followers, 55 repos" which is some random GitHub user, not Notion Labs. The actual Notion Labs GitHub org has thousands of followers. A paying customer would lose trust seeing this.
The DuckDuckGo check correctly returned "did not reference notion.so" as NONE, so that one was honest — but the Wikipedia mismatch is a meaningful bug.
The "517 words" recommendation to "expand to 1,200–2,000 words" is also questionable for a hero homepage. SaaS homepages rarely run that long; the audit conflates marketing-page conventions with article conventions. The advice to add author bylines to a homepage is again misapplied for this page type.
The recommendations are also nearly identical to what Stripe got — same "lead with definition," "add FAQ," "add Last Updated," "add author byline," "cite 2025/2026 stats." For a tool charging brands, the recommendations should feel more tailored. Right now, two very different companies (a fintech homepage with 1,634 words and a SaaS homepage with 517 words) get a checklist that's about 80% identical. That's a productization weakness — it suggests the engine is largely template-driven rather than truly analyzing each page.
Prompt Simulation Review (AI Visibility Score: 14)
The simulation result was striking: Notion scored 14/100, vs. Stripe's 45. Mention rates were ChatGPT 50%, Claude 33%, Gemini 20%, Perplexity 40% — but cited rate was 0% across every single engine. Notion was mentioned in every relevant prompt about AI workspaces but never cited. That's a meaningful, actionable finding for Notion's marketing team.
The competitor tracking worked but with a serious bug
The Citation Gap table showed notion.so 0%, asana.com 0%, coda.io 0%, clickup.com 0% — but with a strange "insufficient data" label appearing under ChatGPT for the three competitors and not for Notion. That's the kind of inconsistency that erodes trust in a paid tool. Either all four domains had data or none did; the asymmetric labeling suggests a parser issue where ChatGPT responses for competitor-domain matching weren't being processed the same way as Notion's. Worth fixing.
Two engines errored out late in the run
Both Gemini and Perplexity returned 429 rate-limit errors on the final prompt ("pricing and limits for enterprise AI workspaces"). The tool exposed the raw error JSON to the user ({"error":{"code":"RATELIMIT_EXCEEDED"...}}) which looks unprofessional. A paying customer should see "We couldn't reach Gemini for this prompt — try again later" rather than a raw API error blob. The tool also counted these errored queries in the Visibility Score denominator, which is what dragged Notion's overall score down to 14. If 17% of Gemini and 17% of Perplexity queries errored, the score is meaningfully understated.
The most-mentioned brands panel revealed a problem
The competitive landscape that surfaced — peoplemanagingpeople.com ×3, mindstudio.ai ×3, read.ai ×2, atlassian.com ×2, teamwork.com ×2, slack.com ×2, gumloop.com ×2, fast.io ×2 — is dominated by content sites (peoplemanagingpeople.com is a media/listicle site, mindstudio.ai is a smaller player) rather than Notion's actual competitors. That's actually the most useful insight in the entire report: the LLMs are citing roundup articles and SEO-optimized listicles when answering "best AI workspace" queries, not the homepages of Notion, Asana, or ClickUp. The tool surfaces this data but doesn't interpret it. A paying customer would benefit hugely from a callout like "Your category is dominated by listicle sites — consider sponsoring placement on peoplemanagingpeople.com or building your own ranked comparison content". Right now, that insight is buried in a chip cloud.
Reading the per-prompt responses
The actual model outputs were genuinely useful. For "best AI workspace for small product teams," Notion shows up as the #1 pick on ChatGPT and Claude, but for "best tools for automating team workflows," it's missing entirely (Zapier, Make, Power Automate dominate). For "pricing and limits for enterprise AI workspaces," ChatGPT and Claude both lead with OpenAI's Workspace Agents — Notion isn't mentioned. That's a real positioning insight: Notion is winning the "workspace" framing but losing the "automation" and "agent pricing" framings. A good simulation tool would highlight this gap automatically. Currently the user has to read all 24 responses themselves to find it.
Overall Take on the Tool as a Paid Product
After two audits, here's my honest assessment of the value-for-money question:
The Prompt Simulation is the standout feature — the per-prompt model outputs, the competitor citation gap, and the "most-mentioned brands" data are genuinely hard to get elsewhere and worth paying for. This is the part I'd lead with on the pricing page. The audit itself feels more like a packaged checklist that any AEO consultant could replicate. The recommendations are largely identical across very different sites, which suggests the tool's intelligence is more in the rules engine than in true page-level analysis.
For a paying customer, the credibility issues are the biggest risk. The Wikipedia disambiguation error on Notion (resolving to "Notion EP" instead of the software company), the wrong GitHub org match, the asymmetric "insufficient data" labels in the competitor table, the raw JSON error blobs, and rate-limit errors counting against the score would all create skepticism on a sales call. These are all fixable but they need fixing before this tool can charge enterprise prices.
I'd also reconsider the headline AEO score. Notion got 50, Stripe got 61, but Stripe got 45 in the simulation while Notion got 14. The two scores don't correlate, which is confusing — the audit score and simulation score appear to measure different things, but they're both presented as the headline number on their respective screens. Either harmonize them or be much clearer that audit-score = "how well-formatted is your page for AI" while simulation-score = "how often do you actually appear in AI answers." Right now, a customer with a 61 audit and 14 simulation might reasonably ask "so am I doing well or poorly?" — and the tool doesn't answer that.
The strongest pitch for this tool is: "Run the simulation, find out what brands LLMs cite instead of you, and use that as your content roadmap." That's a real product. The audit checklist is the table-stakes part — useful, but not the differentiator.
